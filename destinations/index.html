The stage of the Great Migration

Maasai Mara

Big cats on every horizon, the thunder of 1.5 million migrating wildebeest, and dawn balloon flights over golden grasslands — the Mara is not a park you visit; it's a story you step inside.

The Mara is a stage, and it never stops performing. Between July and October, more than 1.5 million wildebeest, zebra and gazelle thunder north from the Serengeti in the planet's greatest overland migration — drawing a full cast of lions, leopards, cheetahs and crocodiles in their wake. Outside those months the show doesn't stop; the Mara's resident big cats are among the most studied — and most filmable — on Earth.

We plan the Mara in two flavours: the public reserve, with its classic river crossings and low fees, and the bordering private conservancies, where night drives, walking safaris and near-empty plains cost more and deliver more. A dawn balloon flight — drifting over hippos and giraffes as the grass turns gold — remains the single most requested add-on we book, and rightly so.

Elephants beneath Kilimanjaro

Amboseli

Vast herds of tuskers strut across the dust with Africa's tallest mountain floating behind them. Amboseli's swamps and salt flats deliver the continent's most iconic elephant photography.

Amboseli is the portrait studio of African wildlife. Meltwater from Kilimanjaro seeps into a chain of emerald swamps that anchor vast, unhurried elephant families — including the legendary big tuskers — against a backdrop of Africa's tallest mountain, floating over the heat haze like a rumour of another world. Photographers raise their shutters here with good reason.

The park is compact and easily walkable-in-a-day, from the elevated views of Observation Hill to dusty elephant dust-baths and Maasai manyattas on the buffer lands beyond. Combine two nights here with a cultural visit arranged through the community — it's the gentlest, most photogenic introduction to southern Kenya safari country.

Ten kilometres of powder-soft sand

Diani & the Coast

Turquoise shallows, dhow sails on the horizon and barefoot beach bars — Kenya's south coast is where safari-weary travellers go to exhale, snorkel the reef and do absolutely nothing, beautifully.

Diani is the beach East Africa brags about: ten unbroken kilometres of powder-soft coral sand, reef-protected shallows in a dozen shades of blue, and Colobus monkeys swaying in the coastal forest behind the dunes. Kite-surfers ride the steady trade winds; snorkellers drift above the reef; everyone else simply exhales.

Beyond the sand, the south coast rewards the curious — Wasini Island's coral gardens and seafood feasts, dhow sundown cruises, Shimba Hills' rainforest and the historical weight of Gede and Fort Jesus further up the coast. It pairs perfectly with any safari: three nights of plains, then three nights of powder.

A sea of pink on the Rift Valley floor

Lake Nakuru

Flamingo flocks that turn the shoreline rose, white and black rhino browsing the flats, and pelicans wheeling over soda water — Nakuru is the Rift Valley's greatest theatre, small enough to see in a day.

Lake Nakuru is the Rift Valley's greatest theatre in miniature. The soda lake's algae blooms draw flamingo flocks so vast they turn the shoreline rose-pink; white rhino and the critically endangered black rhino browse the flats under 24-hour sanctuary protection; and from Baboon Cliff the whole panorama — water, birds, escarpment — assembles itself in a single 270-degree sweep.

At roughly 190 square kilometres, Nakuru rewards a focused day rather than a marathon. Most of our travellers pair it with Lake Naivasha's hippo pools and Crescent Island walks, or fold it into a Rift Valley overnight between Nairobi and heavier safari destinations. Birders, meanwhile, book it on the strength of 450-plus recorded species alone.

Kenya's wild northern frontier

Samburu

Reticulated giraffes, Grevy's zebras and long-necked gerenuks — species the south never sees — framed by doum palms and red-earth hills under a huge northern sky.

Samburu is what the Mara was fifty years ago — wild, warm-hued and wonderfully uncrowded. The Ewaso Nyiro river stitches the reserve together, and its forested banks concentrate elephants, leopards and the famous Special Five: Grevy's zebra, reticulated giraffe, beisa oryx, gerenuk and Somali ostrich, none of which you'll see south of the equator line.

The Samburu people are the park's co-authors — many guides and lodge staff come from nearby villages, and a well-run cultural visit is a conversation, not a performance. Photographers love the clean light and red-earth contrasts; safari veterans love the slower pace and the feeling of having the north of Kenya almost to themselves.

Africa's rooftop, astride the equator

Mount Kenya

Glaciated pinnacles rising straight out of bamboo forest and moorland — climb to its trekking summit, circle its lakes, or fish the trout streams in its foothills.

Mount Kenya is the mountain Kilimanjaro's brochures forget to mention — colder, greener, dramatically quieter and, many trekkers argue, more beautiful. Its twin summits of Batian and Nelion belong to technical climbers, but Point Lenana at 4,985 metres is a tough-but-achievable trekking summit that needs no ropes — just lungs, guides and three well-planned days.

The ascent reads like a continent compressed: equatorial rainforest with colobus monkeys, then bamboo, then giant lobelia moorland, then rock and ice. Sirimon and Chogoria are our favourite routes — Chogoria's descent past the tarns is the single most scenic walk in the country. Add a night at a Rift Valley lodge before and after; your knees will write you thank-you notes.

Turquoise calm inside a marine park

Watamu

Powder-white coves inside the Watamu Marine National Park, mangrove creek boardwalks and a 700-year-old ruined city in the forest next door.

Watamu is the coast at its gentlest — a cluster of white-sand coves inside a protected marine park, where the snorkelling is Kenya's best and the pace is set by fishermen and tide tables. Green turtles nest on these beaches; Mida Creek's mangrove channels offer sunset boardwalks and kayak drifts; and paddleboard yoga happens unironically.

Inland, the lost Swahili city of Gede hides in the forest — coral-stone walls swallowed by fig trees, with a resident herd of golden-rumped elephant shrews and one of the coast's best small museums. Watamu suits travellers who want Diani's beauty at half the volume: perfect for slow weeks, families and repeat visitors who've already ticked the obvious boxes.

Kenya's largest wild — red dust and legend

Tsavo

Nearly twenty-two thousand square kilometres of lava flows, sky-wide plains and red-dust elephants, scene of the famous man-eaters of 1898.

Tsavo is the kind of vast that recalibrates you. Kenya's largest protected complex — split into East and West — rolls out red-earth plains dusted by elephants the colour of the soil itself, delivered under an endless sky. Mzima Springs is its miracle: a fern-lined oasis where you can watch hippos and crocodiles gliding past an underwater viewing window fed by Chyulu Hills rainwater.

Then there's the legend: the 1898 man-eating lions that stopped the Uganda Railway cold, memorialised at Tsavo Station's small museum and in a hundred campfire retellings. Tsavo pairs naturally with the coast — the Nairobi–Mombasa line and road run right past it — and suits road-trippers who want big distances, big herds and big skies.

Kenya's oldest living Swahili town

Lamu

Donkey lanes, dhow sails and seven centuries of Indian Ocean history on an island where the car never caught on.

Lamu Old Town is East Africa's oldest continuously inhabited Swahili settlement — a UNESCO-listed maze of coral-stone houses, carved plaster and rooftop terraces where the loudest traffic is the sea. Donkeys carry everything; the call to prayer and the creak of dhow rigging set the rhythm. You walk slowly here, because everything about the island asks you to.

Days divide between Shela's twelve-kilometre beach — soft, sleepy and perfect for barefoot lunches — and sunset dhow sails through the channel, with Swahili seafood at small rooftop tables to finish. November's cultural festival and the Maulidi celebrations are worth planning around; otherwise, come to do gloriously little, beautifully.

  • UNESCO Old Town walks
  • Shela beach days
  • Sunset dhow sails

The journeys we're asked for most once Kenya is ticked: the spice island and the civilization that outlasted time.

Tanzania

Spice island of the Indian Ocean

Zanzibar

Stone Town's carved doors and spice markets, then powder-white beaches and dhows at anchor. Zanzibar finishes every East African journey with a long, luminous exhale.

Zanzibar finishes every East African journey with a long, luminous exhale. Stone Town is the continent's most walkable museum — carved doors, spice-scented alleys, the former slave market memorial and rooftop cafés where the call to prayer rolls across the rooftops. Then the island lets its hair down at Nungwi and Kendwa, where powder-white sand meets a sea that changes colour by the hour.

We layer the island to your pace: spice-farm tours and Prison Island's giant tortoises for the curious, sunset dhow sails for the romantic, and diving on the Mnemba reef for the waterborne. Flights connect Nairobi to Zanzibar in barely an hour, which is why half our safari travellers end their journeys here.

Egypt

Five thousand years, still standing

Cairo & Giza

Stand beneath the last surviving Wonder of the Ancient World, cruise the Nile like the pharaohs did, and bargain through Khan el-Khalili's lantern-lit alleys — Egypt is time travel with Wi-Fi.

Egypt is time travel with Wi-Fi. Stand beneath the last surviving Wonder of the Ancient World at Giza, then float past five thousand years of civilisation on a Nile dinner cruise the same evening. Cairo's Grand Egyptian Museum holds Tutankhamun's glittering hoard; the city itself — honking, fragrant, kaleidoscopic — is an attraction in its own right.

We build Egypt journeys with the same care as our safaris: Egyptologist guides who actually answer your questions, pyramid-view hotel rooms that make wake-up calls redundant, and a Luxor add-on for those who want the temples too. Direct flights from Nairobi make Cairo one of our most requested long-haul journeys — and one of the most word-of-mouth.
